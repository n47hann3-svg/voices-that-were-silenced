Language Is Identity

Language is more than communication. It carries memory, culture, history, and identity from one generation to the next.

Throughout American history, schools and government institutions often used language suppression as a method of assimilation. Indigenous children and Mexican-American students were punished for speaking their native languages.

Schools as Tools of Assimilation

Indigenous boarding schools and English-only educational systems attempted to erase cultural identity through forced language loss.

Many students experienced punishment, humiliation, and isolation for speaking Spanish or Indigenous languages in school.

Timeline of Language Suppression

Across generations, educational systems were used to erase cultural identity through language assimilation policies.

1800s
Indigenous boarding schools begin expanding across the United States, separating children from their families and forbidding native languages.
Late 1800s
Students were punished for speaking Indigenous languages or Spanish in classrooms and residential schools.
1900s
“English-only” education policies reinforced assimilation and cultural erasure.
1960s–1970s
Civil rights and ethnic studies movements challenged assimilation systems and demanded bilingual and culturally inclusive education.
Today
Indigenous educators and activists continue revitalization efforts through schools, archives, digital preservation, and community programs.

Voices Punished in the Classroom

Across schools in America, students were often punished for speaking Spanish or Indigenous languages.

Educational systems became tools of assimilation designed to erase cultural identity and replace it with enforced conformity.

For many communities, language loss became intergenerational trauma.

Resistance and Healing

Today, Indigenous communities, educators, and activists are revitalizing endangered languages through schools, digital archives, community programs, and storytelling.

Preserving language is an act of resistance, survival, and healing.

Healing Through Language

Across America, communities are reclaiming languages once targeted by assimilation systems. Preserving language has become an act of cultural survival, healing, and resistance.

Community Education

Indigenous and bilingual schools help younger generations reconnect with cultural identity and ancestral languages.

Digital Preservation

Audio archives, online dictionaries, and language apps preserve endangered languages for future generations.

Storytelling & Oral History

Elders, educators, and activists continue passing down stories, traditions, and spoken language through community projects.

Cultural Resistance

Speaking suppressed languages publicly challenges historical systems of erasure and affirms cultural identity.
